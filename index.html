Understanding Fractional Distillation, Rectification & Stripping

Fractional distillation is a critical chemical engineering unit operation used to separate binary or multi-component liquid mixtures into their pure components based on differences in their boiling points (relative volatility) [1, 23]. The process takes place in a vertical column where vapor rises and liquid descends, creating counter-current intimate contact on multiple trays or plates [1, 24].

A standard distillation column with a middle feed is divided into two main zones:

  • Rectifying (Enriching) Section: Located above the feed plate [1]. In this section, the vapor is enriched in the more volatile component (Heptane) as it rises. Liquid reflux from the condenser flows down, washing out the less volatile component (Octane) [1, 24].
  • Stripping (Despojo/Agotamiento) Section: Located below the feed plate [1]. Here, the descending liquid is stripped of the more volatile component by the rising vapor generated in the reboiler, leaving a bottoms product concentrated in the heavy key [1, 25].

This simulator models the mass and equilibrium behaviors on a single plate. Under the Sorel-Lewis assumption (constant liquid and vapor molar flow rates), we can use linear interpolation of the vapor-liquid equilibrium (VLE) data alongside the plate mass balance to determine all key composition streams entering and leaving a plate [3, 25, 30].

Step-by-Step Mathematical Solution

Step 1: Linear Interpolation of VLE Data

Based on entered vapor composition y = 0.8000, we bracket the value in the VLE table between:

y₁ = 0.8004 (x₁ = 0.6465) and y₂ = 0.8565 (x₂ = 0.7314)

Using linear interpolation:

\(x = x_1 + \frac{x_2 - x_1}{y_2 - y_1} (y - y_1)\)

Calculated Liquid mole fraction: x = 0.6465

Step 2: Single-Plate Mass Balance

Applying the operating line (Sorel-Lewis simplified mass balance) for the rectifying section:

\(y_{n+1} = \frac{L}{V} x_n + \left(1 - \frac{L}{V}\right) z_D\)

\(y_{n+1} = (0.75)(0.6465) + (1 - 0.75)(0.9800) = 0.7299\)

Visualizing the Equilibrium

The blue curve shows the Vapor-Liquid Equilibrium (VLE) for the Heptane-Octane system [26]. Since Heptane is the lighter component (higher vapor pressure), the equilibrium curve lies comfortably above the diagonal 45-degree reference line (\(y=x\)) [25, 27].

The red dot dynamically shows your current calculated operating tray coordinates \((x_n, y_n)\).

The green dashed line represents the local column operating conditions. By plotting these curves, chemical engineers can count the theoretical plates necessary for a complete separation, known as the McCabe-Thiele method [25, 27].
